Acute kidney injury and death in severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria: An individual patient data meta-analysis.

PLoS Med · Watson JA et al. · DOI

Systematic review / MAMulticenterInfection & fever
Clinical 7/10 Research 8/10
Why it matters
Current severe malaria AKI thresholds are based on adult values and miss many children; ED-usable age-based criteria could improve risk stratification.
Design
Individual patient data meta-analysis of 22,663 patients from 14 prospective severe malaria studies, using Bayesian penalised spline models with age interactions.
Key findings
Among 16,441 included patients, 84% were children under 15. Admission creatinine >3 times age/weight-predicted baseline and urea >10 mmol/L were associated with >5% mortality. In children, urea predicted death better than creatinine and correlated with parasite biomass. The new thresholds identify 4-8 times more children with severe malaria AKI at high risk of death.
Bottom line
ED clinicians should use age-based creatinine fold-change or a urea threshold of 10 mmol/L, rather than adult absolute values, to recognize severe malaria AKI in children.

Methods: Individual patient data meta-analysis of 14 prospective severe malaria studies with Bayesian spline modeling.

Why these scores

Clinical: Provides new age-based creatinine and urea thresholds that can improve ED risk stratification of children with severe malaria.

Research: Large IPD meta-analysis using Bayesian spline models to derive age-specific prognostic thresholds, a method uncommon in PEM research.

Balanced Crystalloids Versus Normal Saline for Fluid Resuscitation in Pediatric Septic Shock: An Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.

Pediatr Emerg Care · Yousaf AA et al. · DOI

Systematic review / MAResuscitation & critical care
Clinical 8/10 Research 5/10
Why it matters
Fluid choice in pediatric septic shock is a common ED decision; this meta-analysis provides updated evidence on balanced crystalloids versus normal saline.
Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (10,308 participants) comparing balanced crystalloids with normal saline in children with septic shock.
Key findings
No significant difference in mortality (3.8% vs 4.0%) or acute kidney injury (19.7% vs 29.6%). Balanced crystalloids reduced need for renal replacement therapy (1.3% vs 1.9%) and hyperchloremia (32.3% vs 49.9%).
Bottom line
Balanced crystalloids may be preferred over normal saline for pediatric septic shock resuscitation, though mortality benefit is not demonstrated.
Why these scores

Clinical: Updated meta-analysis shows balanced crystalloids reduce RRT and hyperchloremia without mortality benefit, likely to influence fluid choice in pediatric septic shock.

Research: Standard meta-analysis with sensitivity analyses, updates prior evidence.

Bystander CPR Type and Outcomes After Pediatric Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest.

JAMA Netw Open · Obara T et al. · DOI

Prospective cohortMulticenterResuscitation & critical carePrehospital & EMS
Clinical 6/10 Research 6/10
Why it matters
This large national study challenges the idea that compression-only CPR is enough for children, showing that adding rescue breaths was linked to better survival and neurologic outcomes after pediatric cardiac arrest.
Design
Retrospective cohort study using the nationwide All-Japan Utstein Registry, including 26,785 children aged 17 years or younger with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest from 2005 to 2023.
Key findings
Compression-only CPR rose from 14.6% in 2005 to 55.1% in 2023, while rescue-breathing CPR fell from 41.7% to 10.4%; the proportion receiving no bystander CPR plateaued around 35% after 2013. Neurologically favorable 30-day survival was 3.6% with no bystander CPR, 9.5% with compression-only CPR, and 16.0% with rescue-breathing CPR. Compared with compression-only CPR, rescue-breathing CPR was associated with higher rates of neurologically favorable survival (adjusted RR 1.71), 30-day survival (1.51), and prehospital return of spontaneous circulation (1.62).
Bottom line
Rescue breathing remains important in pediatric resuscitation, even as compression-only CPR has become more common.

Methods: Nationwide prospective registry cohort with multinomial logistic regression and modified Poisson regression.

Why these scores

Clinical: Large national registry data support rescue-breathing CPR over compression-only CPR for pediatric OHCA, reinforcing current pediatric resuscitation guidance.

Research: Standard registry analysis with temporal trend modeling and modified Poisson regression; robust but not methodologically novel.

Climate Vulnerability, Historical Redlining, and Children's Mental Health Care Use.

JAMA Netw Open · Lee EK et al. · DOI

Cross-sectionalMulticenterMental & behavioral healthEquity & access
Clinical 2/10 Research 6/10
Why it matters
This study links climate vulnerability and historical redlining to children's mental health care use, showing that structural inequities may compound climate-related stress in marginalized neighborhoods.
Design
Cross-sectional study using New York State all-payer hospital data from 2017 to 2022 for children under 18, with census-tract-level analysis of climate vulnerability, redlining status, and mental health visits.
Key findings
Among 553,134 mental health visits (mean age 7.2 years; 51.6% male), children in the highest climate-vulnerability areas had higher outpatient visit rates than those in the lowest (RR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.72-2.27), while ED rates were not significantly higher (RR, 1.14; 95% CI, 0.96-1.35). In historically redlined neighborhoods, high vulnerability was associated with increased outpatient (RR, 1.47; 95% CI, 1.44-1.51) and ED (RR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.02-1.09) use; in nonredlined areas, outpatient use was lower (RR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.45-0.49).
Bottom line
Children living in historically redlined, climate-vulnerable neighborhoods have higher mental health care utilization, highlighting geographic and structural disparities relevant to climate preparedness and mental health services.

Methods: Cross-sectional analysis of New York State all-payer hospital administrative data (2017-2022) with census-tract-level Poisson regression.

Why these scores

Clinical: Population-level cross-sectional study of ED and outpatient mental health visits; provides background on inequities but no direct clinical decision.

Research: Novel linkage of climate vulnerability and historical redlining to pediatric mental health utilization; standard cross-sectional design.

Modified pneumonia risk score using lung ultrasound for the prediction of radiographic pneumonia in children.

Am J Emerg Med · Jarjour J et al. · DOI

Diagnostic accuracyPOCUSRespiratoryImaging
Clinical 6/10 Research 6/10
Why it matters
Childhood pneumonia is hard to diagnose clinically, and many children with moderate risk are sent for chest x-rays. Adding lung ultrasound may help identify who truly needs imaging.
Design
This was a diagnostic accuracy study using a convenience sample from a prospective lung ultrasound registry of children aged 6 months to 18 years seen in the emergency department with respiratory complaints who had both chest x-ray and lung ultrasound.
Key findings
Of 275 children, 58 (21%) had radiographic pneumonia. Lung ultrasound consolidation was strongly associated with pneumonia (OR 28.68; 95% CI 9.98–82.43). The PRS plus lung ultrasound had better discrimination than PRS alone (AUC 0.85 vs 0.70; P<0.001). The proposed algorithm reclassified 120 (44%) moderate-risk patients as high or low risk.
Bottom line
Adding lung ultrasound to the pneumonia risk score improves prediction of radiographic pneumonia and may reduce chest x-ray use, especially in moderate-risk children.

Methods: Prospective lung ultrasound registry convenience sample; diagnostic accuracy study with logistic regression.

Why these scores

Clinical: Prospective single-center diagnostic accuracy study suggests LUS can improve pneumonia risk stratification and reduce CXRs in the ED.

Research: Standard diagnostic accuracy design with a clever reclassification analysis; needs external validation before changing practice.
